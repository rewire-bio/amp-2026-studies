1. The question in one picture

A genome differs from the reference at 4 to 5 million sites (1000 Genomes 2015). A diagnostic pipeline filters them by gene panel, frequency and consequence to a short list of candidates, then a scientist reviews them in order (Smedley 2015; Whiffin 2017). Scores decide that order. In our test cases the list has a median of 235 rare variants (rare-disease/results/results.json). Genomic foundation models promise better scores. Do they put the diagnosis nearer the top?

    s --> r["Scientist reviews<br/>from the top"]

Every study here is a version of one test: does adding a foundation-model score move the diagnostic variant up the list, compared with the scores labs already use?

2. Primer: the ideas the studies rest on

2.1 What a variant score is

A variant score is a number a computer program gives each variant. A higher number means the variant is more likely to damage a gene. Labs use these numbers in two ways:

  1. To sort a patient's candidate list, so the most likely culprits are read first (Smedley 2015).
  2. As evidence when classifying a variant. In the ACMG/AMP rules, a damaging prediction counts as code PP3 and a harmless one as BP4 (Richards 2015; Pejaver 2022).

Our studies test the first use: sorting.

flowchart LR
    v["A variant, for example<br/>a BRCA1 SNV"] --> p["Predictor"]
    p --> n["A number:<br/>higher = more damaging"]
    n --> u1["Sorts the<br/>candidate list"]
    n --> u2["Evidence for<br/>classification (PP3)"]

Sources: CADD (Kircher 2014; Schubach 2024), SpliceAI (Jaganathan 2019), REVEL (Ioannidis 2016), AlphaMissense (Cheng 2023), GPN-MSA (Benegas 2025), GPN-Star (Ye 2026), AlphaGenome and AVI (Avsec 2026; Cheng 2026), Evo 2 (Brixi 2026). The 0.7 s per variant is from our own H100 run: 2.9 windows per second, two windows per variant (rare-disease/evo2/README.md).

Most scores come as a table. The developers run their model once on every possible variant and publish the results as a file. For SNVs that is about 9 billion rows. A lab then looks each variant up by its position and allele. This takes milliseconds, needs no GPU, and gives the same answer every time. Labs already use the established scores this way: CADD publishes a score for every possible SNV (Schubach 2024), and REVEL and AlphaMissense for every possible missense change (Ioannidis 2016; Cheng 2023). In our studies, SpliceAI also came from a table (the SpliceAI scores in CADD's annotations); for indels we ran SpliceAI ourselves.

A foundation model is a large model trained on DNA sequence (and, for AlphaGenome, on lab measurements of molecular activity), not on lists of disease variants. Three of the ones we test publish a genome-wide table, so a lab can use them exactly like CADD: GPN-MSA, GPN-Star and AlphaGenome AVI.

AVI is not a pure foundation-model score. It combines AlphaGenome's predictions with AlphaMissense, conservation and the variant's consequence, using a model trained to tell rare from common variants in gnomAD (Cheng 2026).

Evo 2 has no genome-wide table. EVEE publishes scores built on Evo 2, but only for the about 4.25 million variants already in ClinVar (Pearce 2026). Most of a patient's rare variants are not in ClinVar. To score those, a lab has to run Evo 2 itself on a GPU, which costs money and, as we found, can give slightly different answers on different GPUs.

2.3 Why we test whole cases, not single variants

Most papers test a score on a big list of variants already labelled pathogenic or benign, and report one number for how well it separates them (the AUROC). That is not the lab's question. The lab's question is: for this patient, is the variant that causes the disease near the top of the list?

The two can disagree. In ClinVar, 98% of nonsense variants are pathogenic and 0.1% of synonymous ones (Pearce 2026). So a score can get a high AUROC just by recognising the type of variant. That does not help a lab whose list already contains many variants of the same types. Variant-level tests can also look better than they are when the score was trained on the same variants (Grimm 2015), or because some genes have far more pathogenic variants than others (Lu 2026).

So we build cases instead. Each case is one person's list of rare variants with one known disease-causing variant added. We sort the list by a score and record where the disease-causing variant lands.

flowchart LR
    subgraph v["Variant-level test"]

        a["Thousands of variants<br/>pathogenic vs benign"] --> au["AUROC"]
    end
    subgraph c["Case-level test"]

We report the result as top 1 and top 5: the share of cases in which the disease-causing variant was ranked first, or among the first five. Top 1 means the score found the answer outright. Top 5 means the answer was on the first screen a scientist reads.

2.4 Combining scores: what does a new score add?

A lab never sorts by one score alone. It looks at several together: CADD, SpliceAI for splicing, REVEL or AlphaMissense for missense changes, and the type of variant. So the useful question is not "how good is the new score?" but "does it help on top of the scores a lab already has?"

To answer that, several scores have to be turned into one sort order. We use logistic regression, a standard statistical model. It looks at cases where we know the answer and learns how much weight to give each score. For example, it can learn to trust SpliceAI for intronic variants and REVEL for missense ones.

We build two versions and compare them on the same cases:

  1. Lookups combined. CADD, SpliceAI, REVEL, AlphaMissense and the variant type. This stands in for what a lab has today.
  2. Lookups plus one new score. The same, plus one foundation-model score.

The difference between them is what the new score adds. On the 200 SNV cases, for example, the lookups combined rank the disease-causing variant first in 59.5% of cases; adding AVI raises that to 67.0%, a gain of 7.5 points.

flowchart LR
    l["Lookups combined<br/>CADD + SpliceAI + REVEL<br/>+ AlphaMissense + variant type"] --> b["Ranked first<br/>in 59.5% of cases"]
    l2["Lookups combined<br/>+ AVI"] --> n["Ranked first<br/>in 67.0% of cases"]
    b & n --> g["AVI adds 7.5 points<br/>(95% interval -0.5 to +15.5)"]

Two safeguards keep this honest:

  • The model never ranks a case it learned from. The cases come from 50 people. We learn the weights on the cases of four fifths of them and rank the cases of the remaining fifth, then rotate until every case has been ranked (cross-validation).
  • Every gain has a 95% interval. We resample the 50 people 1,000 times and recompute the gain each time (a bootstrap). If the interval includes zero, we cannot be sure the new score helps.

Numbers: rare-disease/results/results.json; method: rare-disease/README.md.

2.5 Four ways this kind of test can mislead, and how we checked each

flowchart TB
    t1["Circularity:<br/>the score was trained on the<br/>same labels it is tested on"] --> c1["Name it as a limit;<br/>test AVI on chromosomes<br/>it was not trained on"]
    t2["Easy backgrounds:<br/>common variants a pipeline<br/>would have removed"] --> c2["Rerun with a gnomAD<br/>frequency filter"]
    t3["Luck:<br/>the best of several scores<br/>on 200 cases looks too good"] --> c3["Test again on 500 new cases<br/>without refitting the model"]
    t4["Weak baseline:<br/>a new score 'helps' a<br/>combination that was poor"] --> c4["Compare with all<br/>established scores<br/>combined, fitted the same way"]
  1. Circularity. If a score was trained on the same variants it is tested on, it will look too good (Grimm 2015). AVI's developers trained it on some chromosomes and not others (Cheng 2026), so we checked its ranking on the chromosomes it never saw (rare-disease/REPLICATION.md).
  2. Easy backgrounds. Our backgrounds are rare in 1000 Genomes, but a real pipeline also removes anything common in gnomAD (Whiffin 2017). Leaving those in could make the task too easy for some scores, so we reran with a gnomAD frequency filter.
  3. Luck. Test five scores on 200 cases and one will look best partly by chance. So we froze the fitted model ("locked") and tested it on 500 new cases from 50 different people.
  4. Weak baseline. A new score can look useful only because the comparison was poor. So the baseline is all the established scores combined by the same method, not one score on its own.

4. The studies, one card each

4.1 Rare-disease case benchmark (the main study)

Question. Put one known disease-causing variant into a real person's rare variants. How often does each score rank it first, or in the top 5? Does adding a foundation-model score to the established lookups help?

Why. This is the closest public approximation of a lab's candidate list. It uses real backgrounds, real ClinVar diagnoses and the scores labs already have.

Folder. rare-disease/, with the follow-up checks in rare-disease/REPLICATION.md.

flowchart LR
    person["1000 Genomes person"] --> rare["Rare variants in<br/>panel genes (~235)"]
    cv["ClinVar P/LP, 2+ stars"] --> dx["Diagnostic variant"]
    rare & dx --> case["Case"]
    case --> scores["9 scores and<br/>combined models"]
    scores --> rank["Rank of the diagnosis"]

Four studies share this design: 200 SNV cases, 120 indel cases, 140 inherited cancer cases, and a 500-case replication with 50 new people (rare-disease/README.md). Backgrounds come from 1000 Genomes (Byrska-Bishop 2022), genes from PanelApp (Martin 2019) on MANE transcripts (Morales 2022), and diagnoses from ClinVar (Landrum 2018).

Each single score on its own, 200 SNV cases, share of cases with the diagnosis in the top 5:

    y-axis "Cases with diagnosis in top 5 (%)" 0 --> 100
    bar [27, 26.5, 43, 48.5, 51.5, 54.5, 57.5, 87, 92]

AM is AlphaMissense, GPN-Star the vertebrate model, and Lookups the established scores combined. REVEL and AlphaMissense score only missense variants, so they miss every other diagnosis. AVI is the best single score; the established lookups combined are better still. Source: rare-disease/results/results.json, strata.all.scores.

Each bar is uncertain by 5 to 8 points either way, so read them as a group. The gain of about 6 to 8 points survives new cases and a normal frequency filter. Under the strict filter it disappears. That filter shrinks each list to a median of 35 to 51 variants, and the lookups alone already rank the disease-causing variant first in 74 to 80% of cases, so there is little left for any new score to improve.

Findings in one line each (all from rare-disease/results/):

  • The established lookups, combined, do most of the work.
  • AVI is the one foundation-model table whose gain holds on new cases.
  • GPN-MSA's +6 point gain on the first 200 cases did not replicate.
  • On indels, Evo 2 adds +16 points at top 1, or +8 after a frequency filter.
  • On a realistic short list, little room is left for any new score to help.

Read first: Richards 2015 (how labs classify variants), Pejaver 2022 (score thresholds as evidence), Martin 2019 (PanelApp), Byrska-Bishop 2022 (the 1000 Genomes backgrounds), Cheng 2026 (the AlphaGenome Atlas preprint and its own rare-disease test), Grimm 2015 (circularity).

4.2 Evo 2 on the case variants

Question. What does it take to score every case variant with a model that has no genome-wide table, and does its score help?

Why. Evo 2 is the largest openly released DNA foundation model (Brixi 2026), and a lab could only use it on a patient's variants by running it.

Folder. rare-disease/evo2/

flowchart LR
    v["16,357 case variants"] --> w["Two 8,192 bp windows each<br/>reference and variant"]
    w --> h["One rented H100<br/>pinned container"]
    h --> d["Score = change in<br/>mean log-likelihood"]
    d --> b["Into the case benchmark<br/>as 'Evo 2 7B'"]
    h --> rc["Receipts: GPU, image digest,<br/>timings, cost"]

Two runs took about 3.5 hours of pod time and cost about USD 14. Throughput was 2.9 windows per second (rare-disease/evo2/README.md). On SNVs, Evo 2 alone was among the weaker scores (43% top 5, results.json). On indels, where few tables exist, it was the strongest single score (92.5% top 5) and added the largest gain to the lookups (results_indel.json).

Read first: Brixi 2026 (Evo 2), Arc Institute's BRCA1 zero-shot notebook (the scoring method followed here).

4.3 BRCA1 case ranking

Question. On one gene with lab-measured function, does Evo 2 put the loss-of-function variant near the top of a simulated case?

Why. It was the first case-level test. Saturation genome editing gives a functional label for 96.5% of possible SNVs in 13 BRCA1 exons (Findlay 2018), so it does not depend on ClinVar.

4.4 Evo 2 pipeline

Question. What does adding an Evo 2 score to a lab pipeline involve in practice?

Why. A score is only useful if it can run inside a validated, reproducible pipeline.

Folder. evo2-pipeline/

flowchart LR
    vcf["Case VCF"] --> n["Normalise"] --> a["Add CADD,<br/>SpliceAI"] --> w["Windows"] --> s["Evo 2<br/>(GPU or replay)"] --> r["Rank the case"]

It is a Nextflow pipeline with two profiles. replay runs on a laptop with recorded H100 scores and has been run end to end. gpu uses the same scoring code but has not been run end to end through Nextflow. The GPU, cost and reproducibility findings shown in the talk, including scores that drift between GPU types, come from the separate evo2-cloud-operations study.

Source: evo2-pipeline/README.md.

Read first: Di Tommaso 2017 (Nextflow), NVIDIA's cuBLAS results reproducibility notes (why GPU results can differ between GPU types).

4.5 EVEE charts

Question. None of our own. This folder redraws two figures from Goodfire's EVEE preprint for the slides.

Why they matter here. They show the trap from section 2.3. In ClinVar, a variant's consequence alone predicts much of its label, so variant-level benchmarks reward scores that learn consequence. Within each consequence, AUROCs are lower (for CADD v1.7, 0.99 overall against about 0.79 to 0.95 within a consequence) and the gaps between methods change (Pearce 2026; data from goodfire-ai/evee-manuscript).

6. Glossary

Term Meaning
ACMG/AMP The standard framework for classifying variants into five classes: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign, benign (Richards 2015)
PP3, BP4 ACMG/AMP evidence codes for computational predictions supporting pathogenic or benign
P/LP Pathogenic or likely pathogenic
ClinVar review stars How well supported a ClinVar classification is. 2 stars: two or more submitters with criteria and no conflict; 3: expert panel; 4: practice guideline
Background The rare variants of one 1000 Genomes person, into which a diagnostic variant is inserted
Top k Share of cases with the diagnostic variant in the first k positions
Lookups combined Logistic regression on CADD, SpliceAI, REVEL, AlphaMissense and consequence class
Locked A model fitted on the first 200 cases and applied unchanged to the 500 new ones
Bootstrap interval A 95% range from resampling the background people 1,000 times
AF, grpmax AF Allele frequency in gnomAD. grpmax is the highest frequency among the large, non-bottlenecked ancestry groups (for genomes: afr, amr, eas, nfe, sas)
Zero-shot Using a model's own likelihoods as a score, without training on labels
Log-likelihood delta How much less likely the model finds the variant sequence than the reference
SGE Saturation genome editing: nearly every possible SNV in a region made in cells and its effect measured (Findlay 2018)
AUROC How well a score separates two groups across all thresholds; 1 is perfect, 0.5 is chance
