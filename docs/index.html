1. The question in one picture

A rare disease genome has millions of variants. A diagnostic pipeline filters them to a few hundred candidates, then a scientist reviews them in order. Scores decide that order. Genomic foundation models promise better scores. Do they put the diagnosis nearer the top?

flowchart LR
    g["Patient genome<br/>~5 million variants"] --> f["Pipeline filters<br/>panel genes, rare,<br/>protein or splice effect"]
    f --> c["Candidate list<br/>~100 to 300 variants"]
    c --> s["Sort by scores"]
    s --> r["Scientist reviews<br/>from the top"]
    r --> d["Diagnosis<br/>(if it is near the top)"]

    old["Established scores<br/>CADD, SpliceAI,<br/>REVEL, AlphaMissense"] -.-> s
    new["Foundation-model scores<br/>GPN-MSA, GPN-Star,<br/>AlphaGenome AVI, Evo 2"] -.-> s

    style new fill:#fde2ef,stroke:#df007d
    style old fill:#e3eefa,stroke:#005eb8

Every study here is a version of one test: does adding a foundation-model score move the diagnostic variant up the list, compared with the scores labs already use?

2. Primer: the ideas the studies rest on

2.1 A variant score

A variant effect predictor gives each variant a number: higher means more likely to damage a gene. Labs use these numbers as one line of evidence in ACMG/AMP classification (PP3 and BP4) and to sort candidates.

flowchart LR
    v["A variant, for example<br/>a BRCA1 SNV"] --> p["Predictor"]
    p --> n["A number:<br/>higher = more damaging"]
    n --> u1["Sorts the<br/>candidate list"]
    n --> u2["Evidence for<br/>classification (PP3)"]

2.2 Two kinds of score, and two ways to get one

    style fm fill:#fde2ef,stroke:#df007d

A foundation model learns from raw DNA sequence without disease labels and is then used for many tasks. Some publish genome-wide score tables (GPN-MSA, GPN-Star, AVI), so a lab can look them up like CADD. Evo 2 has no genome-wide table. EVEE publishes scores built on Evo 2, but only for the 4.2 million variants already in ClinVar, so it cannot score most of a patient's rare variants. For those, a lab has to run Evo 2 itself, which raises cost and reproducibility questions.

2.3 Why rank cases, not variants

Most papers report how well a score separates pathogenic from benign variants across a whole dataset (AUROC). A lab asks something different: within one patient, is the diagnosis near the top? A score can look good on AUROC by learning that nonsense variants tend to be pathogenic, yet not help inside a case where the candidates already passed a consequence filter.

flowchart LR
    subgraph v["Variant-level test"]
        direction TB
        a["Thousands of variants<br/>pathogenic vs benign"] --> au["AUROC"]
    end
    subgraph c["Case-level test"]
        direction TB
        b["One patient's ~235 rare variants<br/>1 is the diagnosis"] --> rk["Rank of the diagnosis"] --> tk["Top 1 / top 5 / top 10<br/>across many cases"]
    end
    v -.->|"can mislead because<br/>consequence alone<br/>predicts labels"| c

Top k is the share of cases in which the diagnostic variant is in the first k positions. Top 1 asks whether the score finds the answer outright; top 5 asks whether the answer lands on the first screen.

2.4 Combining scores, and "what does it add?"

No lab uses one score alone. The studies fit a simple model (logistic regression) on the established scores plus the variant's consequence class: "lookups combined". Then they add one foundation-model score and measure the change. That change, not the score on its own, is the result that matters to a lab.

flowchart LR
    l["Lookups combined<br/>CADD + SpliceAI + REVEL<br/>+ AlphaMissense + consequence"] --> b["Baseline<br/>top 1 = 59.5%"]
    l2["Lookups combined<br/>+ one new score"] --> n["New<br/>top 1 = 67.0%"]
    b & n --> g["Gain = +7.5 points<br/>with a 95% interval"]

The model is fitted with cross-validation grouped by person, so no case is scored by a model that saw it. Intervals come from resampling the background people (a bootstrap).

2.5 Four ways a benchmark can fool you, and the check for each

    t2["Easy backgrounds:<br/>common variants a pipeline<br/>would have removed"] --> c2["Rerun with a gnomAD<br/>frequency filter"]
    t3["Winner's curse:<br/>the best of several scores<br/>on 200 cases looks too good"] --> c3["Replicate on 500 new cases<br/>with the model locked"]

4. The studies, one card each

4.1 Rare-disease case benchmark (the main study)

Question. Put one known disease-causing variant into a real person's rare variants. How often does each score rank it first, or in the top 5? Does adding a foundation-model score to the established lookups help?

Why. This is the closest public approximation of a lab's candidate list. It uses real backgrounds, real ClinVar diagnoses and the scores labs already have.

Folder. rare-disease/, with the follow-up checks in rare-disease/REPLICATION.md.

flowchart LR
    person["1000 Genomes person"] --> rare["Rare variants in<br/>panel genes (~235)"]
    cv["ClinVar P/LP, 2+ stars"] --> dx["Diagnostic variant"]
    rare & dx --> case["Case"]
    case --> scores["9 scores and<br/>combined models"]
    scores --> rank["Rank of the diagnosis"]

Four studies share this design: 200 SNV cases, 120 indel cases, 140 inherited cancer cases, and a 500-case replication with 50 new people.

Each single score on its own, 200 SNV cases, share of cases with the diagnosis in the top 5:

xychart-beta
    title "Top 5, single scores, 200 SNV cases (%)"
    x-axis ["REVEL", "AM", "Evo 2", "GPN-MSA", "CADD", "SpliceAI", "GPN-Star", "AVI", "Lookups"]
    y-axis "Cases with diagnosis in top 5 (%)" 0 --> 100
    bar [27, 26.5, 43, 48.5, 51.5, 54.5, 57.5, 87, 92]

AM is AlphaMissense, GPN-Star the vertebrate model, and Lookups the established scores combined. REVEL and AlphaMissense score only missense variants, so they miss every other diagnosis. AVI is the best single score; the established lookups combined are better still.

What adding AlphaGenome AVI to the lookups gains at top 1, as the checks got stricter:

xychart-beta
    title "AVI added to the lookups: gain at top 1 (points)"
    x-axis ["A", "B", "C", "D", "E"]
    y-axis "Gain (points)" 0 --> 10
    bar [7.5, 6.2, 8.0, 7.4, 0.8]

A: the first 200 cases. B: 500 new cases, model locked. C: 200 cases with a gnomAD AF filter below 0.1%. D: 500 new cases, locked, with that filter. E: 500 new cases, locked, with the strict grpmax filter below 0.1%.

The 95% intervals are about ±7 points, so read the bars together. The gain holds on new cases and with a pipeline's frequency filter. Under a strict filter, lists shrink to a median of 34 to 49 variants. The lookups alone then rank the diagnosis first in 70 to 79% of cases, which leaves little room for any score to add.

Findings in one line each:

  • The established lookups, combined, do most of the work.
  • AVI is the one foundation-model table whose gain holds on new cases.
  • GPN-MSA's +6 point gain on the first 200 cases did not replicate.
  • On indels, Evo 2 adds +16 points at top 1, or +8 after a frequency filter.
  • On a realistic short list, little room is left for any new score to help.

Read first: Richards 2015 (how labs classify variants), Pejaver 2022 (score thresholds as evidence), Martin 2019 (PanelApp), Byrska-Bishop 2022 (the 1000 Genomes backgrounds), Cheng 2026 (the AlphaGenome Atlas preprint and its own rare-disease test), Grimm 2015 (circularity).

4.2 Evo 2 on the case variants

Question. What does it take to score every case variant with a model that has no genome-wide table, and does its score help?

Why. Evo 2 is the best-known DNA foundation model, and a lab could only use it by running it.

Folder. rare-disease/evo2/

flowchart LR
    v["16,357 case variants"] --> w["Two 8,192 bp windows each<br/>reference and variant"]
    w --> h["One rented H100<br/>pinned container"]
    h --> d["Score = change in<br/>mean log-likelihood"]
    d --> b["Into the case benchmark<br/>as 'Evo 2 7B'"]
    h --> rc["Receipts: GPU, image digest,<br/>timings, cost"]

Two runs took about 3.5 hours of pod time and cost about USD 14. Throughput was 2.9 windows per second. On SNVs, Evo 2 alone was among the weaker scores (43% top 5). On indels, where few tables exist, it was the strongest single score (92.5% top 5) and added the largest gain to the lookups.

Read first: Brixi 2026 (Evo 2), Arc Institute's BRCA1 zero-shot notebook (the scoring method followed here).

4.3 BRCA1 case ranking

Question. On one gene with lab-measured function, does Evo 2 put the loss-of-function variant near the top of a simulated case?

Why. It was the first case-level test. Saturation genome editing gives a functional label for almost every possible SNV in the tested BRCA1 exons, so it does not depend on ClinVar.

Folder. brca1-case-ranking/

flowchart LR
    s["3,893 BRCA1 SNVs<br/>with assay labels"] --> l["823 loss of function"]
    s --> f["3,070 functional"]
    l --> c["Case: 1 LOF<br/>+ 100 functional"]
    f --> c
    c --> r["Rank: CADD, SpliceAI,<br/>REVEL, Evo 2, combinations"]
xychart-beta
    title "BRCA1 cases (K = 100): loss-of-function variant in the top 5 (%)"
    x-axis ["REVEL", "SpliceAI", "Evo 2", "CADD", "C+S", "C+S+Evo 2"]
    y-axis "Cases (%)" 0 --> 100
    bar [30.4, 37.2, 48.1, 54.9, 62.0, 65.0]

C+S is the type-aware CADD + SpliceAI combination. Evo 2 adds about 3 points to it, but against the best existing score for each variant type no gain is detected. The study also showed that how scores are combined matters as much as which scores are combined. That lesson shaped the rare-disease design.

Read first: Findlay 2018 (BRCA1 saturation genome editing).

4.4 Evo 2 pipeline

Question. What does adding an Evo 2 score to a lab pipeline involve in practice?

Why. A score is only useful if it can run inside a validated, reproducible pipeline.

Folder. evo2-pipeline/

flowchart LR
    vcf["Case VCF"] --> n["Normalise"] --> a["Add CADD,<br/>SpliceAI"] --> w["Windows"] --> s["Evo 2<br/>(GPU or replay)"] --> r["Rank the case"]

It is a Nextflow pipeline with two profiles. replay runs on a laptop with recorded H100 scores and has been run end to end. gpu uses the same scoring code but has not been run end to end through Nextflow. The GPU, cost and reproducibility findings shown in the talk, including scores that drift between GPU types, come from the separate evo2-cloud-operations study.

Read first: Di Tommaso 2017 (Nextflow), the cuBLAS reproducibility notes (why GPU results can differ).

4.5 EVEE charts

Question. None of our own. This folder redraws two figures from Goodfire's EVEE preprint for the slides.

Why they matter here. They show the trap from section 2.3. In ClinVar, a variant's consequence alone predicts much of its label, so variant-level benchmarks reward scores that learn consequence. Within each consequence the scores separate much less well.

6. Glossary

Term Meaning
ACMG/AMP The standard framework for classifying variants as pathogenic, benign or uncertain
PP3, BP4 ACMG/AMP evidence codes for computational predictions supporting pathogenic or benign
P/LP Pathogenic or likely pathogenic
ClinVar review stars How well supported a ClinVar classification is; 2+ stars means multiple submitters agree or an expert panel
Background The rare variants of one 1000 Genomes person, into which a diagnostic variant is inserted
Top k Share of cases with the diagnostic variant in the first k positions
Lookups combined Logistic regression on CADD, SpliceAI, REVEL, AlphaMissense and consequence class
Locked A model fitted on the first 200 cases and applied unchanged to the 500 new ones
Bootstrap interval A 95% range from resampling the background people 1,000 times
AF, grpmax AF Allele frequency in gnomAD; grpmax is the highest frequency in any one ancestry group
Zero-shot Using a model's own likelihoods as a score, without training on labels
Log-likelihood delta How much less likely the model finds the variant sequence than the reference
SGE Saturation genome editing: every possible SNV in a region made in cells and its effect measured
AUROC How well a score separates two groups across all thresholds; 1 is perfect, 0.5 is chance
